Rent or buy? 1,000 American futures

A U.S. version of the CBC News rent-or-buy simulator. Each run follows three households for up to 30 years — one buys with a 30-year fixed mortgage, one buys with an adjustable-rate mortgage, one rents and invests — through 1,000 futures rebuilt from 25 years (2001–2025) of real U.S. home prices, rents, inflation, interest rates and market returns. It shows probabilities, not predictions.

Each dot is one simulated future, colored by the household with the highest net worth after selling the home and paying taxes.

How the simulator works

Three households, one budget

All three start with the same cash: the down payment plus buyer closing costs. The renter invests that cash on day one. Every month, the simulator computes each household's housing outlay; whichever pays less invests the difference in a stock and bond mix. This mirrors the CBC design (savings from cheaper housing are invested), extended both ways: when rent overtakes an owner's payment, the owner invests the gap instead.

1,000 futures from 25 years of history

Each future is built by stringing together blocks of consecutive historical years (five by default) drawn at random from 2001–2025. A sampled year carries all of its values together — that metro's home price change, national rent growth, inflation, S&P 500 and 10-year Treasury returns, and the change in the 1-year Treasury yield — so relationships between them, like the 2008 crash coinciding with falling rates, are preserved. The history window is set in config/assumptions.yaml. Like the CBC tool, the record covers about 25 years, so earlier housing cycles such as the early-1990s decline are not in the sample.

Mortgages

The fixed-rate owner locks today's rate for 30 years and, if enabled, refinances once the simulated market rate is a set number of points lower, rolling the cost into the balance and keeping the remaining term. The market rate moves by the year-to-year changes in Freddie Mac's 30-year average from the sampled history. The ARM owner pays the starting rate for the fixed period, then resets once a year to the index plus the margin, limited by the per-year and lifetime caps and floored at the margin. The index starts at today's 1-year Treasury yield and moves by the same year-to-year changes as the sampled history. Most new U.S. ARMs now reset every six months off SOFR; the 1-year Treasury is used because it has the full 25-year history. PMI is charged when the down payment is under 20% and stops when the balance reaches 78% of the original price, the automatic-termination point under the Homeowners Protection Act of 1998.

Taxes

When enabled, owners get the federal benefit of itemizing only to the extent mortgage interest (on up to $750,000 of debt) plus property and state taxes (under the SALT cap) exceed the standard deduction. The 2026 standard deduction is $32,200 joint or $16,100 single and grows with inflation. The SALT cap follows current law: $40,400 in 2026, rising 1% a year through 2029 and returning to $10,000 in 2030; the high-income phase-down is not modeled. Home sale gains up to $500,000 (joint) or $250,000 (single) are excluded under IRC §121, and investment gains are taxed at your capital-gains rate when everything is sold at the end. Dividend taxes during the holding period, state taxes on gains and depreciation recapture are not modeled.

What it leaves out

  • The FHFA all-transactions index tracks repeat sales and refinance appraisals of single-family homes with conforming mortgages. It is smoother than sales-only indexes and can understate swings, and condos can behave differently.
  • Rent growth is the national CPI rent index, the only rent series with a long, consistent history. Local rents can run hotter or cooler, and CPI rent reflects all leases, not just new ones.
  • No refinancing, moving costs, renovations, special assessments, or the value of stability, rent control or the freedom to move.
  • Maintenance is a flat share of home value. Experts quoted by CBC noted real repair costs are often higher, especially when something big fails.
  • Insurance and property tax defaults are statewide averages. Florida, Texas and other storm-prone states often cost far more to insure; use a real quote.

Defaults that are assumptions, not data

Closing costs, PMI, maintenance, renter's insurance, other selling costs, the ARM starting-rate spread, margin and caps, the refinance trigger and cost, fund fees and the stock-bond split are round-number starting points. Each one is labeled as an assumption in config/assumptions.yaml. Replace them with your own figures.

Data sources

  1. Home prices: FHFA All-Transactions House Price Index for each metro (FRED series ATNHPIUS…Q), fourth-quarter to fourth-quarter changes. The exact series for each location is shown in the history section and listed in data/series.lock.json.
  2. Rent growth: BLS CPI-U Rent of primary residence, U.S. city average (FRED CUUR0000SEHA), change in annual averages.
  3. Inflation: BLS CPI-U all items, not seasonally adjusted (FRED CPIAUCNS), December to December.
  4. Stock and bond returns: Aswath Damodaran, NYU Stern, Historical Returns on Stocks, Bonds and Bills (S&P 500 with dividends; 10-year Treasury), kept in data/manual/market_returns.csv.
  5. Interest rates: Freddie Mac PMMS 30-year fixed (FRED MORTGAGE30US) and the 1-year Treasury constant maturity (FRED GS1 and DGS1), Federal Reserve H.15.
  6. Home values and rents: Zillow Research ZHVI and ZORI metro files.
  7. Property tax: U.S. Census Bureau, American Community Survey 1-year, aggregate real estate taxes (B25090) ÷ aggregate home value (B25082) by state. California uses the Prop 13 base rate of 1%.
  8. Home insurance: Freddie Mac research, 2023 premiums per $1,000 of home value.
  9. Agent commissions: Clever Real Estate 2025 survey, reported by HousingWire.
  10. Tax parameters: 2026 standard deduction and SALT cap (P.L. 119-21), $750,000 mortgage-interest limit, IRC §121 exclusion.
  11. Method: Nael Shiab, CBC News, "Is it better to buy or rent?", October 8, 2026.
